About

I am a CIFAR AI Safety Postdoctoral Fellow at the University of Toronto, where I study compathy: computation and empathy, taken together.

I am affiliated with the Department of Psychology's Work and Play Lab and the Schwartz Reisman Institute for Technology and Society. I received my Ph.D. in Computer Science and Engineering from the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, and have since held research positions at the University of Waterloo, McMaster University, and the University of Toronto.

Two questions

  1. RQ1How could empathy inform the design of better computational tools and models?
  2. RQ2How could technology impact, and ideally reinforce, humans' capacity for positive empathy, without losing sight of moral reasoning?

Three pillars

Compathy is the thread that runs through everything I do; these three pillars are how I follow it, each bringing computation and empathy a step closer to one another.

Responsible AI
Embedding fairness, transparency, and accountability into how intelligent systems learn and decide.
Human-centred design
Treating people as active contributors, so that human goals, values, and context shape computational intelligence.
Human experience
Understanding trust, comprehension, and reliance on AI through real, repeated, everyday interaction.

Compathy sits where human-computer interaction meets computational intelligence, and its questions cut across computer science, engineering, psychology, philosophy, and medicine, so the work is collaborative by necessity. Across all of it, the aim is to close the growing gap between people and the intelligent systems that increasingly shape their lives.

Teaching

I think of teaching as a way of taking students seriously.

Most of what a course transmits is not its content, which students can find on their own, but a way of thinking: how to notice a question, how to stay with it before answering, and how to tell a sound argument from a fluent one. My part is to make that kind of thinking visible, and then to step back far enough that students can practise it themselves, make their own mistakes, and find that they can recover from them. A course has gone well when students leave more curious than they arrived, and more confident that their curiosity is worth following.

Mentoring asks for the same stance with more patience: listening before advising, and treating a student's question as a real question rather than a gap to be filled. The courses below are a record of where I have tried to work this way.
